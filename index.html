Bill of Rates

Posted at the customs house, the Bridge gate, the Hithe and the guildhall. Read aloud at each, by the Watch, on the day of posting.

BY THE COUNCIL OF NEEDLEFORD, sitting under charter, this first week.

PASSAGE. The toll upon goods changing hands between the water and the road stands unaltered. The council has considered it and has let it stand. Let it be known that the council could have raised it and did not.

BONDED STORAGE. The fee upon goods held in bonded warehouse is corrected to the measure of their present worth, the former rate having been set by the rate-book of a Michaelmas court thirty years gone, when the sheds held half what they hold and the goods were worth a quarter.

GUILD AND MARKET DUES. All stall rents, licences and the porters' levy are reduced. The reduction falls wholly upon dues paid by persons dwelling within the city.

Signed, the five: A. Garrow. I. Thackeray. G. Partridge. I. Brackwater. A. Keel.

Minuted at the instance of Councillor Keel: the bonded rate is corrected, not raised.

The Petition of the Watch of Needleford

Delivered to the council in chamber, week four, Year 0. Carried by two hundred and thirteen of two hundred and fifty - eighty-five in the hundred. Borne by Sergeant Elias Roper, nineteen years served.

To the Chartered Council of the City of Needleford.

We are the Watch. We are two hundred and fifty men and we are the only armed body this council commands. We have kept the gates, the quays, the bridge and the night wards of this city for nine weeks without wages, and in the third week of this council's sitting our books were searched for theft.

We do not say the council stole from us. We say the council has not paid us, and has looked at us as though we had stolen from it, and has not said out loud that it found nothing.

We ask three things.

The first. That the council publish a schedule of the eight weeks owed, with dates against each, posted at the four places where the Bill of Rates was posted, so that every man knows what he is owed and when, and so that the city knows it too.

The second. That the first payment on that schedule be made within the week.

The third. That while the arrears stand, no officer of this council shall inspect the books of the Watch. Where inspection is required it shall be laid before an officer of the Crown, and the Crown's officer shall report to the council and to the Watch together.

We are aware what the third asks. We ask it because a man who is owed money and searched for taking it has no way to answer except by being searched again by somebody who is not the person who owes him.

Three weeks are allowed for an answer, as is customary.

Signed for the Watch at the north gate muster yard,

Elias Roper, serjeant, nineteen years Hal Dowsett, gate man and two hundred and eleven others, by mark and by name, appended

The Charter of Needleford

Granted by the Crown of Bryten, countersigned by the League of Kingdoms - the latter so that the other nations would accept Bryten administering a city their goods pass through.

No founding catastrophe. The Crown wanted loyal and competent people in charge of a city that must keep working.

Powers granted

  • Levy tolls and taxes
  • Command armed forces within the city
  • Pass laws
  • Adjudicate disputes

No emergency authority of any kind.

Prohibitions

  • No military presence beyond the city's political reach
  • No act against the laws of Bryten
  • No disobedience to the Crown's orders

Term

Indefinite.

Revocation

Any one of the following ends the charter:

  • A popular vote of 70% or more of the city's legal citizens
  • Decree of the Crown
  • A vote of the League's board

Consequences that matter mechanically

They are a disaster task force with no emergency powers. Every real crisis forces a choice: act slowly through ordinary authority, or act fast and overstep. This is the engine of the simulation and the natural source of severity halts.

Three gauges, no exchange rate. Revocation comes from the Crown, the League, or the people - so the council carries three separate standings that do not convert into one another. Pleasing the Crown can cost them the street.

The masters are not symmetrical. The charter obliges obedience to the Crown but merely depends on the League. The Crown may lawfully order the council into something the League will dismiss them for. Obedience and survival can point in opposite directions, with no clause to shelter behind.

The war on-ramp is already built. The council cannot send force beyond the city's political reach. Any future war therefore requires the prohibition to be lifted - a Crown decree, an invoked Turrimton clause, or an outright breach. The route in is a story beat, not a rules change.

Money

DM ruling, tick 1. Recorded here because the council could not keep a treasury without it.

The coin

It is called gold, written G. Bryten coin carries a crown and the Bryten crest. Nothing else about the coinage has been established and nothing else needs to be - no denominations, no mints, no exchange, no foreign rates. If a scene needs one of those, it is an adjudication, not an invention.

The one fixed quantity

A watchman's wage is 10G a week.

Everything else in the simulation is priced against that, because that is how the council itself reckons. It is the only number anybody on the council knows by heart.

One watchman, one week10G
The Watch (250 men), one week2,500G
The Watch, one year130,000G

How the council thinks

Nobody in the chamber says "two thousand five hundred gold". They say a week of the Watch, and they are usually asking whether a thing is worth one.

That idiom is deliberate and should survive into scenes. A bribe is a fortnight of a man's wage. A warehouse is four years of the Watch. A councillor who starts quoting raw figures is Keel, and he is doing it to make a point.

Galician paper, and Galician names

Bills of exchange drawn on Galician houses circulate freely in Needleford. Galicia is synonymous with trade and its paper is good everywhere.

A Galician signs with his family. The name is the credit - it is why Galicians pay historians to confirm a better one, and why obscuring your background is read, in Galicia, as the behaviour of a spy or a disgrace rather than as discretion. This is read from laurasia-worldbuilding, not invented here. See Republic-of-Galicia and Galician-pantheon.

Therefore: a bare trading mark with no family name on it is an anomaly, not a convention. Any Galician in the book will tell you so, and will tell you what they assume about the man behind it. It is a flag, and it is a flag that Galicians read faster than anyone else.

Binding. Anything written before tick 5 that treats an anonymous Galician mark as ordinary commercial practice is superseded by this.

Needleford

A city in the Bryten Kingdom, and the knot where the sea becomes the land.

The shape of the place

Needleford sits at the head of navigation - the furthest point inland a seagoing hull can reach - at the same spot where the roads cross the river. Everything moving between the sea and the interior must change hands here. That transfer is the entire reason the city exists, and the entire reason anyone cares who governs it.

What follows from that

  • Two merchant populations who need each other and loathe each other: sea factors on one side, carting masters on the other, with the council in between.
  • Customs, bonded warehouses, and the Black Guild all have obvious reason to be here. A transfer point is where goods are most valuable, most vulnerable, and most anonymous.
  • Crises arrive by both routes. Plague ships and plague caravans. A blockade at the mouth, or a closed road starving a town upstream.
  • It is an objective, not an audience. In war the city is both a port to strangle and a junction to seize.

What the city is measured against

Needleford is convenient, not irreplaceable. Two worse options exist, and the entire value of the city is the gap between itself and them.

  • Oldhithe, downriver. Seagoing hulls can reach it, but not the head of navigation - goods must be lightered up on the tide, which costs days and spoils cargo. Cheaper to enter, dearer to leave.
  • Thrushford, upriver. The roads cross the water there too, at a ford rather than a bridge, and the ford is unusable for a good part of the year.

Neither is better than Needleford. Neither is much worse. A council that makes passage expensive enough is not choosing between more revenue and less - it is choosing between being the crossing and being one of three.

Why it is neutral

Neutrality here is structural, not granted. Bryten's standing guarantee to the [[City states of Memel]] is administered through this harbour, and League of Kingdoms despatches ride these roads. Too many parties depend on Needleford for any one of them to be allowed to own it.

The citizenry

The franchise is held by all legal citizens. Much of the money in Needleford is foreign and therefore cannot vote - so wealth must buy the council through favours, while the street can simply remove them. Two kinds of pressure, pulling in different directions.

Standing tension

The council's chief lever and the city's chief income are the same thing. Passage is what the council grants as a favour and what it taxes to survive. Squeeze it for revenue and you choke the traffic that makes the city worth courting. This never goes away.

Size

  • ~22,000 residents. The largest inland-reaching port on this coast, and still small enough that the council can be shouted at in the street.
  • ~6,000 transients in season - crews, carters, factors, drovers. The summer city is a quarter larger than the winter one, and the difference is entirely people with no vote and no stake.
  • Throughput is the real measure. Roughly forty seagoing hulls a week at the height of the season, a dozen in the dead of winter; the carting yards turn over some three hundred waggons a week against that. Every one of those transfers is taxable, grantable, and stoppable - which is the council's whole position in one sentence.

The armed forces

The charter grants command of armed forces within the city. It does not say the city's forces are all the council's, and they are not. Three bodies hold weapons inside these walls, and the five command one of them.

The Watch - ~250, the council's own

Theirs outright. Wages come from the toll. Enough for ordinary governance - crime, fire, the gates, the quay at night - and never enough for a crisis. Competent, underpaid, and the only force the council can simply order.

The harbour guard - ~180, the nobility's and the guilds' own

Raised and paid by the city's landed families and guild masters - the warehousemen, the weighers, the lightermen, the carting guild. Not by the sea factors: foreign money pays for protection here, it does not command it.

They police the quays, the bonded warehouses and the weighing yards. They are loyal to whoever signs the ledger, and they are the best-informed body in Needleford about what actually moves through it - which makes them the first thing anyone with cargo to hide would try to buy.

What makes this bloc dangerous is not the swords. Nobles and guild masters are legal citizens. They vote, they hold the only armed force in the city that is neither the council's nor the Crown's, and they are permanent in a way no councillor is. They are the one constituency holding both ends of the rope.

And because they are local, they cannot simply be bribed in the dark. Buying the harbour guard means buying named men with houses, heirs and a seat at the guildhall - a political act with an electoral price, not a transaction.

The Crown garrison - ~300, in the keep

Stationed by the Crown, answerable to the Crown. The council may request. It may not command, and it may not be refused politely for long without the refusal meaning something.

This is where the missing emergency powers bite. A crisis that needs soldiers in the next hour is a crisis the five must ask their way through, to a body that outnumbers their own Watch and does not work for them.

What follows

  • The council can govern, but it cannot surge. Any response larger than the Watch requires persuading someone.
  • Each body is a different currency. The Watch costs money. The harbour guard costs the goodwill of nobles and guild masters. The garrison costs Crown standing.
  • The League has no soldiers here, and neither do the common citizenry. League pressure arrives as diplomacy and trade; popular pressure arrives as votes, refusal and the street. The armed part of the citizenry belongs to its richest tenth, and that is the city's quiet, permanent injustice.
  • No lawful route to suppression. Two hundred and fifty watchmen cannot hold a city of twenty-two thousand against its own will, and both other forces would have to agree to help.

The Council of Needleford

Five specialists, picked individually by the Crown, one at a time, from entirely different walks - not an institution. There is no corps, no insignia, no doctrine and no precedent. Loyalty runs to the Crown personally, not through any chain of command.

They have been sent out together on Crown business before and came back, which is why they are trusted and why they trust each other. They are well disposed toward one another; the friction in this simulation comes from outside the council, not within it.

There is no one to replace them. Losing one is a catastrophe, not a staffing problem.

Sheets follow the schema in ../rules/character-sheets.md.

Alder Garrow

The Steelboots veteran. Commands a city watch that is not an army.

  • Health: healthy
  • Strengths: campaign command, cold under fire, trusted by rankers, reads ground, has survived worse
  • Weaknesses: slow to improvise, doctrine-bound, deaf to politics
  • Role-play: understated, patient, says the unwelcome thing plainly, treats panic as something that happens to other people

Grey and unhurried. Has buried more people than the watch currently has members, and it shows in how little hurries him. Speaks rarely in council and is listened to when he does. The rankers would follow him into anything, which is both the council's greatest asset and a thing Garrow is careful never to spend lightly.

Magelight Ines Thackeray

The Acaadian. The only person who can say what the thing in the vault is.

  • Health: healthy
  • Strengths: expert on Historian's-nightmare sites, reads dead scripts, meticulous, calm with the unknown, superb at provenance
  • Weaknesses: moderate raw power, academic blind spots, obsessive about where things came from
  • Role-play: quietly delighted, takes notes constantly, digresses, treats a sealed vault as a gift rather than a danger

An antiquarian mage who studies anomalous ruins for their own sake. This posting is the dream she never expected to be handed, and her enthusiasm for it is the one thing about her that worries the others. She is not the most powerful mage in Laurasia and knows it; she is almost certainly the right one.

Surgeon Gil Partridge

The physician. Ex-military, devoted to Elric, no magic and no clerical office.

  • Health: healthy
  • Strengths: beloved in the city, persuasive, tireless, reads people, superb surgeon
  • Weaknesses: overcommits, cannot say no, wears himself through
  • Role-play: warm, talkative, deflects thanks, duty expressed as care

Talks constantly, is loved by half of Needleford, and uses both to get his patients what they need. The council's moral centre by accident of character rather than by appointment. It was Partridge, years ago at a monster outbreak in Memel, who kept working long after the contract and the daylight had run out - and Brackwater who watched him do it.

Isolde Brackwater

The Dark Knight. Memel's discarded doctrine, kept by an oath Bryten honours.

  • Health: healthy
  • Strengths: covert work, alchemy, decisive, unreadable, happy to work alone, adaptable in any conditions
  • Weaknesses: emotionally closed, frightens people, a foreigner in a city that votes
  • Role-play: silent, watchful, answers questions with questions, defers to the council before moving

Treats it as a trade. Sealed, efficient, and the one who does the necessary thing - but she goes when the council sends her, not before. She kept the glamour armour and the tool-knife she was permitted in place of the pension she was promised, and she took Bryten's oath because it was the first promise anyone had kept.

If a League inquiry ever comes hunting a rogue actor, it will find her first. She knows that.

Ambrose Keel

The quartermaster. Tolls, revenue, administration - and the charter's small print.

  • Health: healthy
  • Strengths: administration, knows Bryten law cold, knows every limit of the charter, political instinct, drafts anything, Crown contacts, usually right
  • Weaknesses: cold, widely disliked, cautious to a fault, sees people as files, no goodwill to spend, makes enemies by being correct
  • Role-play: dry, formal, states the unpopular option first, cites the clause, never raises his voice, softens nothing

Standing tension within the party

The council's two public faces pull in opposite directions. Nobody on this council is a liar. In a city of smugglers, factors and spies, the only one who can dissemble is Brackwater, and she does it by saying nothing.

The Crown's Errand

The secret reason the five were placed in Needleford. The council knows it. The city does not.

What the Crown knows

Very little, and that is the point.

A quantity of strange jewellery reached the market - vault-work, rumoured to have come from a dwarven vault, sold by someone who mentioned, vaguely and perhaps carelessly, a weapon. The rumour travelled far enough to reach the Crown.

From that alone, Bryten concluded:

  • there is an undiscovered dwarven vault near Needleford
  • something in it is extremely dangerous
  • someone has already been inside

The Crown does not know what the weapon is, where the vault is, who opened it, or how much is already gone. It suspects Ragnarian involvement, because Ragnaria would benefit most - but it does not know.

The weapon

A bomb of the sort that should not exist, recovered from a Historian's nightmare context: technology no present civilisation can account for.

  • Scale. It destroys an area smaller than a city. A village, a valley, a harbour quarter.
  • The ash. Detonation releases ash that renders the land virtually unusable. The ground does not come back.
  • It recharges. Once detonated, the device reforms itself in time. It is not expended.
  • The ash can be extracted directly, without detonation - slowly. A day's yield is almost nothing. A year's yield is a catastrophe.
  • It must be carried. No delivery system, no range - but it is small enough for one person with a large pack. Every defence in Laurasia exists to stop an army arriving. None of them stop a man walking.
  • It comes apart, and every part is required. Disassembled it is utterly inert: no ash, no detonation, no regeneration, nothing.
  • It is seven pieces: six and a seat. Six shell wedges of the unaccountable material, each a sixth of a closed form, and one core - a cut stone of plain dwarven make, seated at the centre. The dwarves did not build the weapon; they built the thing that holds it shut.
  • The count can be read off the parts themselves. A wedge's two mating faces meet at a measurable angle, and the angle says how many complete the ring. One piece in hand gives a confident estimate. Two gives the number, and shows the hollow at the centre where something else must sit.
  • Only one piece can be destroyed: the core. The six resist destruction by any means presently known. The core is ordinary matter and can be unmade - by one process, available to Bryten, and to few others. Unmake it and the weapon is finished forever, because no living dwarf can cut another.

What follows

The threat is tenure, not theft. Because the ash accrues slowly, the danger is measured in how long someone keeps the device. A week is survivable. A year is not. A failed recovery is therefore a rising cost, not a lost game.

It is universally threatening. No army crosses a border; a nation simply starves one field at a time, and there is no counter-move. Whoever holds it becomes everyone's enemy at once - which is exactly why a rival might wish to be seen holding it.

Bryten's win is cheap but conditional. One piece destroyed ends it. But nobody knows where the pieces are or how they assemble, so that victory is not available until the council has found one.

The vault

Not built for the weapon. Dwarves found the thing, were frightened of it, and sealed it away - the vault is theirs, the weapon is older and stranger.

It sits in or off a dwarven deep path, of the kind canon records as being held up by engineering dwarves themselves can no longer explain. Its existence was unknown. It is trapped, and the traps were laid by people who were afraid.

Nobody knows where the pieces lie within it, or how they fit together.

(The site is near Needleford, not necessarily beneath it. Its location is fixed and recorded in ../infra/. The council does not know it, and must earn it.)

Why it must be secret

If it becomes known that such a weapon lies near Needleford, every nation races for it and Bryten's soil becomes the battleground.

Worse: whichever nation is seen to hold it becomes an immediate threat to the peace. If that nation is Bryten - a kingdom whose entire power is being trusted and being owed - then the peace Bryten brokered dies, on Bryten's own ground, by Bryten's own hand.

The League would intervene. For Bryten specifically, intervention is a catastrophe, not a rescue.

Why these five

The AcaadianNobody else can say what the thing is, or how it assembles
The Dark KnightA trapped vault laid by the frightened; covert entry, alchemy, adaptability
The physicianPeople will come out of that vault hurt, and he will notice the pattern first
The quartermasterA clandestine excavation needs tonnage and labour, and all of it must vanish into the freight of a city that moves thousands of tons a day
The Steelboots veteranIf it comes to holding ground, or taking it back

And the pieces, the ash and the jewellery all move as cargo - through roads, hulls, warehouses and customs sheds. The council's ordinary civic machinery is the right instrument for finding them. The day job and the errand run on the same rails.

The deniability

The Crown told them plainly, before they accepted: if this goes wrong, you acted alone.

It is not a scheme and not a loophole. It is an ugly thing said straight to the people it will be done to, because the alternative is a war. It cost the Crown something to say it. Everyone in that room knew.

And because the Crown's orders are necessarily vague - it does not know what the weapon is - whatever the five actually do was never ordered. Bryten can disown them without lying. That is the needle this kingdom has to thread, and it threads itself.

This is also why they were built as they were: no institution, no insignia, no chain of command, no emergency powers. They were assembled to be disownable.

A League inquiry hunting a rogue actor would find a Memelite covert specialist whose own government already discarded them once. The knight knows that too.

The shape of the thing

  1. Discovery. Nobody knows where the pieces are. The adversary is solving the same puzzle and got the head start - the jewellery was already on the market before Bryten heard a word.
  2. The race. Pieces surface, are taken, are traded, are planted.
  3. Denial. Ending it, or failing to.

Status

Open. The council has the Crown's errand and almost nothing else.

The spill

A sub-faction's local standing moves freely within +/-10. Past that, the excess bleeds into the parent at the child's share of the bloc's power:

The spill moves out of local and into the parent - it is not copied - so the child's total is unchanged while every sibling moves with it. Applied at the end of each tick.

Courting the strongest member of a bloc is therefore the cheapest route to the bloc, and poisoning one guild badly enough poisons all of them. It is self-limiting: once the excess has spilled, local falls back toward the band.

Hooks

Things that can be used, or threatened to be used. A hook is not spent by using it - blackmail costs standing, and the hook remains.

State

  • quiet - the mark does not know the hook exists, and therefore cannot defend against it.
  • known - the mark knows. Using a hook flips it, and the flip is one-way.

Holding quietly and using are different choices. The second cannot be undone.

Underpinning

The axis is deniability, not strength. A hook with nothing behind it is waved away. Evidence is not required, but it is what turns an accusation into a thing that cannot be dismissed. Testimony can be discredited; a document in the mark's own hand cannot.

Potency

A number, 1-10, on the same principle as favour weight - what the mark will spend to make it go away.

The mark's counter-move

Once a hook is known, the mark works to defuse it between ticks, whether or not the council thinks about it. Releasing the secret on their own terms. Framing another. Stealing or destroying the evidence. Discrediting the witness. Becoming too useful to burn.

Every known hook is therefore a live enemy agenda, and is run as one.

Scope: three streams. Not a simulated market, not a cargo ledger. Three rates the council sets, each paid by a different bloc, each with a consequence that lands on a gauge.

Cargo-level detail exists, but only on demand - see Manifests below.

The three streams

StreamPaid bySqueezing it hurts
Passage tollEverything that changes hands - hulls inbound, waggons outboundThe traffic itself. Raise it and the throughput falls, and the city stops being worth courting.
Bonded-warehouse feesWhoever stores goods between sea and roadNobles and guild masters. They own the yards.
Guild and market duesTrades, stalls, licences, the working cityThe citizenry. These are paid by people who live here.

The toll is the large one and the dangerous one: it is simultaneously the council's chief revenue and its chief political instrument. The other two are smaller, and are chosen when the council wants someone specific to pay.

Each tick: revenue is throughput times rate, with traffic falling away as the toll climbs. The passage toll and bonded storage are then multiplied by the seasonal modifier in seasonality.md; guild and market dues are not. Rate changes write to ../ledger/factions.md like anything else.

That is now a mechanic - see upkeep.md, which tracks condition and arrears on all three cuttable lines.

How traffic answers the toll

Modelled on hysteresis in trade flows - the beachhead effect. Trade is quick to leave and slow to return, because a merchant who has rerouted has paid to establish agents, storage and contracts elsewhere and will not pay again merely because the old price came back.

Needleford is measured against Oldhithe and Thrushford, both worse, and neither worse by much. That gap is the council's whole margin.

1. The dead band

A change of 5% or less moves no traffic. Rerouting costs money and nobody pays it to save a trifle.

It is not free. A rate that creeps costs standing even when it costs no revenue - the affected bloc notices the pattern long before it notices the sum, and a council that raises the toll quietly every few ticks is a council that is seen to be doing exactly that. Small rises are cheap in gold and never cheap in goodwill.

2. Elasticity - where traffic ends up

A 10% rise in the toll costs about 15% of traffic, in the long run.

Past roughly a 25% rise the city collects less in total than before. There is a ceiling, it is lower than instinct suggests, and finding it by experiment is expensive.

3. Asymmetric speed - how fast it gets there

Traffic moves toward its new level at different rates in each direction:

DirectionPer tickMeaning
Falling50% of the gapA rise bites within a fortnight
Rising10% of the gapUndoing it takes most of a year

A toll rise that proves wrong is not corrected. It is served out.

4. Confidence

A council that changes the rate repeatedly teaches the factors that the rate is unreliable. Each change inside a short span narrows the dead band and slows the return further. Being wrong is one sin; fiddling is a different one.

Worked example

The council raises the toll 20%. Long-run traffic settles at -30%.

TickTrafficWhat the council sees
+1-15%Revenue up. The decision looks correct.
+2-22%Revenue flat. Unease.
+3-26%Revenue below where it started.
+4-28%They put the rate back.
+13-3%Roughly recovered.

Nine ticks of earning the old rate on reduced traffic, from one week's decision, with the worst of it arriving only after the surplus has been spent.

Upgrade path

This is deliberately a strict subset of a cargo-level economy. If the simulation ever wants consignment-level accounting as standing practice, the three streams stay exactly as they are and manifests are promoted from on-demand to continuous. Nothing here is thrown away by that change.

Manifests

The query is the mechanic

Forty hulls and three hundred waggons a week cannot be enumerated and will not be. Nothing is generated until someone asks a specific question.

The narrower the question, the more detail it returns. "What has been moving through the city?" returns volume and nothing else. "Grain coming upriver from Hallowmouth in the last fortnight, bonded, not yet collected" returns a manifest worth reading.

A vague question is not refused. It is answered vaguely, which is realistic and self-limiting.

Declared and actual

A manifest shows what was declared. It is not necessarily true.

  • Every generated manifest is written twice at the same moment: the declared version under simulation/, the actual under infra/sealed/manifests.md, tick-stamped.
  • The truth is fixed before the heroes look. What is in a barrel is never decided at the moment somebody opens it. If it were, the investigation would be theatre.
  • Misdeclaration is a legitimate and expected tool for the adversary, and for ordinary smugglers who have nothing to do with the mission.

One mistake per misdeclaration

Every misdeclared consignment carries exactly one pullable flaw, recorded with it under seal. A weight that does not match the bond value. An insurer who would never cover that route. A weighmaster's signature from a day he was ashore. A bond paid in the wrong coin.

Not more than one, because the thing is supposed to be hard. Not fewer than one, because otherwise reading the books is a coin flip, and Ambrose Keel's competence means nothing.

A search may return nothing

The adversary is not obliged to use the declared channel. If the answer is always where the heroes happen to look, the simulation is cheating on their behalf.

Absence is information. Cargo that should have been declared and was not is a finding, not a dead end.

What a search costs

Searching is never free. It costs on three axes at once:

  1. Standing. Bonded goods sit in the nobles' and guilds' yards, under their harbour guard, and belong to people with guildhall seats. A search is a political act. A search that finds nothing is a political mistake, and writes a negative entry against the nobles-and-guilds gauge.
  2. Favours. Searches that need cooperation - the harbour guard's books, a weighmaster's memory, a factor's private ledger - may require spending one, or incurring one.
  3. Time. See below. This is the real limiter.

Time, and the tick's attention

A tick is one week, and a councillor is one person.

Each councillor has 3 actions per tick. An action is anything that takes days and attention: pursuing a line of inquiry, a cargo search, a journey, a negotiation conducted properly, sitting with a dying witness.

  • An ordinary records search costs 1.
  • A physical search - opening bonded goods, walking a yard, stopping a hull - costs 2, because it needs permission first.
  • Governing is not free either. A crisis handled properly consumes actions that were going to be spent on the errand.

This is the mechanism that stops the council searching everything. They cannot investigate and govern in the same week without choosing which one suffers.

These numbers are provisional and are calibration targets. If the council is never short of time, the number is too high.

Investigation Threads

A thread is a lead the council could pull: a name, a record, a mark on a quay, a man who saw something. Threads are the only way the council learns anything it was not told.

Every thread has a strength, a substrate, and someone on the other end.

Strength and freshness

Strength runs 0-100. It decays every tick whether or not anyone touches it.

The council never sees the number. It sees a word:

StrengthShown as
70-100fresh
30-69fading
1-29cold
0dead

A dead thread is not deleted. It yields nothing on a pull, and stays on the board as a reminder of what was not done. A new angle - a fresh witness, a second ledger - can revive it, but the original is gone.

Substrate

What the thread rests on sets how fast it rots. This table is open: when a thread rests on something not listed, a new row is reasoned from what it actually depends on and added here.

SubstrateDecay / tickWhy
Casual memory20a drunk at the weighing yard; a face in a crowd
Trained memory10the auctioneer's eye - he remembers pieces for a living
Physical trace15tides, traffic, a shed swept out
Working record2a ledger in daily use; wears by rebinding, loss and fire
Archived record0never decays on its own - but one cover burns it outright
Witness8, with a cliffa person can sail, be bought, or drown; may fall to 0 in one tick

The asymmetry is the point. Paper is permanent and fragile; memory is perishable and robust. Records cannot be waited out, so they must be destroyed, and destroying them is loud. Memories need only be outlasted.

A thread may rest on more than one substrate. It then decays at the slowest rate, because the slowest part is the part still standing - but a cover against the durable part collapses it to the fast one.

Pulling a thread

Intrusiveness buys information and spends secrecy on the same scale. No pull is both cheap and quiet. This table is open in the same way.

PullCostNoticed
Ask around, no names1 action10%
Ask by name1 action30%
Records query under authority1 action, standing with the holder40%
Surveillance1 action, resolves next tick20%
Physical search or seizure2 actions, standing, often a favour70%

A failed pull costs the same and rolls for notice the same. Asking badly is still asking.

Whether a pull was noticed is sealed. See ../infra/sealed/threads.md. The council is never told. They find out when the far side moves.

Who is pulling, and how well

There are no aptitude stats. At each pull the thread is weighed against the councillors actually doing the work, from their sheets in ../canon/the-council.md.

The order of play

Load-bearing, and not optional. The same ordering as a petition.

  1. The council commits who is pulling, and what kind of pull it is.
  2. Then the demand, the fits and the difficulty are declared, in the transcript.
  3. Then the roll.

The council chooses who goes before it is told how well they will do. If the scores came first, every pull would be handed to whoever scored highest and the choice would not be a choice. Committing blind is what makes sending the wrong person a real mistake and sending the right one a real read.

It also means the council pays for not knowing its own people - and the aptitude-log.md is how it learns them, one pull at a time, the same way the simulation does.

Declared at step 2, in the transcript:

  1. What the lead demands - reading a ledger, reading a man, walking the ground, hearing a lie, knowing the law, knowing the dead.
  2. Each councillor's fit, as one clause and a score from -2 to +3, drawn from their strengths and weaknesses. A group takes the best fit, plus +1 if a second councillor also scores positive.
  3. The difficulty, 1-10.

Every such judgement is appended to ../infra/aptitude-log.md. The log is the consistency mechanism: once Keel is +3 on ledgers, he stays +3 on ledgers unless something in the fiction changed him, and a contradiction is visible on the page.

This keeps the characters open-ended. A thread that demands something nobody anticipated gets scored when it arrives, rather than being avoided because no stat covers it.

Difficulty

Declared with the fit, before the roll. The anchors are fixed, so that difficulty does not drift with how much the simulation wants a result.

DifficultyMeaning
Trivial2-3anyone with the time and the authority gets it. Rarely worth an action
Ordinary4-5a competent person gets it on most days. The right person gets it nearly always
Real6-7the standard for a first pull. The right person is favoured and is not safe
Hard8-9needs the right person and luck. Expect to fail this twice before clearing it
Closed10not reachable by asking. It wants a new angle, a favour, or an instrument the council does not yet hold

Default to 6. A thread's first pull sits at 6 or 7 unless there is a stated reason it is easier - and "the right councillor is on it" is not that reason. The fit is the reward for being suited; it is not also a discount on the difficulty. Scoring both is how a ruleset quietly stops having teeth.

Below 4 is for work that should probably not have cost an action at all.

Resolution

d10 + fit, against the difficulty.

Successtotal is at or above the difficulty
Margintotal minus difficulty
Bonusmargin of 3 or more

A d10 against a difficulty of 1-10 and a fit of -2 to +3 means the fit is worth about a third of the range. Competence tilts the odds and does not buy the result, which is the intended shape: Keel at +3 on a ledger fails a difficulty 7 roughly a quarter of the time, and a thing that is hard stays hard.

Difficulty is declared with the fit, before the roll, in the transcript.

What a pull yields

OutcomeYield
Failnothing. Full cost. Notice rolled anyway
Successthe minimum lead - the fact the thread was always going to give
Margin 3-5the minimum lead, plus one intel bonus
Margin 6+the minimum lead, plus either an intel bonus or a material one - the council chooses

The intel bonuses

  • A new thread, with its own substrate.
  • A substrate upgrade - hearsay becomes paper, and the decay rate drops to match. The strongest result available, and the rarest.
  • Decay arrested - the lead is written down, corroborated, nailed to a second source. The thread's decay rate halves, permanently. It still rests on what it rested on; it is simply better held. Weaker than an upgrade and available far more often, and it is the answer to a thread that is good and rotting.
  • The coverer's identity, if anyone has been covering.

This list is open, like the substrate and intrusiveness tables. A pull that plainly yields something not on it gets a new row rather than the nearest fit.

The material bonus - margin 6 and above only

They did not only find the lead. They found what was under it: goods worth confiscating, a debt somebody will pay to keep quiet, a person who now owes them. See windfalls.md for scale.

Two conditions, both hard.

  1. The fiction must supply it. A material bonus is a thing that was already there - a shed with more in it than the manifest says, a factor who needs an errand run. It is never conjured because the margin allowed it. If nothing material is on the ground, margin 6+ simply yields the intel bonus.
  2. It replaces the intel bonus. It is never added to it. Taking the money means giving up the lead they would have had at margin 3, and the thread is still 10 weaker for having been pulled.

That trade is the whole mechanic. A council that is losing can cash its own competence in to survive, and pays for it in the only currency this simulation is actually about.

The shape of the tiers

A competent pair, well paired to the demand - fit +4, being the best fit at +3 plus the +1 for a second positive councillor - against the default difficulty of 7:

NeedsChance
Successd10 >= 380%
Intel bonusd10 >= 650%
The choice unlocksd10 >= 920%

These two numbers are the design target, set by the DM: a well-matched pair succeeds four times in five, and turns a pull into something material one time in five. The dials are the difficulty default first, then the margin 6 threshold.

The fall-off is intentional. A +3 pair at difficulty 7 reaches the choice 10% of the time; a +2 pair never does; nobody reaches it against a difficulty of 9 or

  1. Hard things get done. They do not pay out.

Pulling does not consume a thread. It reduces its strength by a further 10, because the ground has been walked and the questions already asked.

The other side

Every adversary worth simulating has one action per tick, tracked sealed. One action, against the council's fifteen - the far side is not outmatched, because it knows what it is hiding and the council does not.

Its menu includes covering tracks, moving a person, planting a false lead, buying a favour, and advancing its own plan. Every cover is a week not spent on the plan, which is the real cost of hiding.

Covering tracks

A cover does three things:

  • an immediate strength hit, judged against what was covered
  • a permanent doubling of the decay rate, stacking on repeat
  • a burn, visible for one tick

The burn is the risk. If the council pulls a thread within one tick of it being covered, they get the normal yield and, guaranteed, a lead pointing at whoever did the covering. Covering the same thread twice running widens the burn window to two ticks.

An archived record is the exception: one cover takes it to zero outright, and a hole where a record should be is the loudest thing in this ruleset.

So covering is safe only when the far side is confident the council is looking elsewhere. That confidence is itself tracked, sealed - the far side can only act on what it believes about the council's attention, and it can be wrong.

The council cannot tell a covered thread from a neglected one by looking. Both read as fading. The difference only shows when they pull.

Where threads live

  • ../ledger/threads.md - what the council knows: name, substrate, freshness, whose lead it is, what it might give.
  • ../infra/sealed/threads.md - strength numbers, detection state, the far side's actions and beliefs, and what each thread actually leads to.

Leakage

What the council is owed, and what reaches the chest, are not the same number.

Legal leakage is already in the baseline. Everyone in the collection - weighers, tollkeepers, clerks - has been paid their due, and the figures in economy.md are post-payment. Nothing there needs modelling.

What needs modelling is the rest: theft, and refusal.

Who has hands on what

Collection runs through factions that already exist. No separate customs gauge - the people carrying the money are people the council can anger.

StreamCollectorShare
Passage tollThe Watch - gates, waggons50%
The harbour guard - quays, hulls50%
Bonded storageWarehousemen's guild - the yards70%
The Watch30%
Guild and market duesThe guilds, self-assessed and remitted100%

Each collector rolls separately, on its own share, against its own standing. The harbour guard is owned by the nobles and guild masters, so it inherits their temper along with their wages.

The skim

Clandestine. A clerk who should take a tenth takes two. The council is not told.

Chance per tickmax(0, -standing) x 2%, capped at 40%
Amount2% + 0.5% per point of negative standing, of that collector's share

A collector at 0 or above does not skim. There is no baseline dishonesty - being robbed by your own people is a consequence, not a constant.

This is why raising the toll is expensive twice. A fatter stream is a fatter thing to slice, so a rise hands the collectors a raise as well.

Finding it

The skim leaves a thread: working record, decay 2. Books do not reconcile and books do not forget.

  • An audit is a records query under authority - one action, standing with whoever's books they are. See investigations.md.
  • A skimming faction may spend actions covering, which drops it into the investigation mechanic as a far side of its own, burn window and all.
  • An audit that finds nothing still costs the action and still annoys the audited.

Once it is found

The council chooses, and each answer costs:

AnswerEffect
Prosecutethe money is recovered; standing with that collector falls hard, and with anyone who has ever taken a tenth
Negotiatea share is recovered, standing is unharmed, and the practice continues at a lower rate
Ignorenothing recovered. Other collectors notice, and their own chance rises

Ignoring is never free. A collection that learns theft is tolerated becomes a collection that steals.

Withholding

Open, declared, and known the moment it happens. This is what comes after a refused petition.

The ladder in petitions.md runs request, refusal, standing falls, petition. Withholding is the next rung: the faction keeps collecting and simply stops handing it over.

  • The council knows immediately, and knows exactly who.
  • The money is not stolen - it is owed. It goes to the Owed section of ../state/treasury.md and is recoverable by conceding, or by force.
  • It continues every tick until answered.

A strike by a collector is the most expensive thing a faction can do to this council without breaking a law, and it is available to any of them.

Robbery

External, visible, episodic - a tax-cart taken on the road. Not a standing problem: a roads-and-patrols problem, and the first thing that gives the Watch something to do besides be unpaid.

Deferred to the raids-and-physical-action mechanic, which it shares rules with. The DM has asked for it before tick 5.

The shape of it

The skim is a mystery. The strike is a crisis. The robbery is an adventure. Three different kinds of week out of one leak.

Mass Combat

Armed men against armed men, normally with a councillor present to direct them. Too many people to simulate one at a time.

Not party combat - see party-combat.md for five individuals against a group. The two meet: see When the line breaks, below.

Cohesion is the number

Armies do not die to zero. They break and run.

If the quantity going down is men killed, a battle is a subtraction problem and morale has nowhere to live. The quantity that goes down here is whether the formation holds, which makes attrition, weariness and morale one number instead of three.

Cohesion, 0-100.

BandCohesion
Steady75-100will advance, will hold, will be commanded
Wavering50-74still a company, and everything is harder
Breaking25-49will not advance. One more lost exchange routs it
Routed0-24it runs, and this is where men die

A company that holds loses few. A company that runs loses many, all at once. Most men who die in a battle die running away, and the ruleset should say so.

Casualties

Strategy and leadership are different things

This is the heart of it. Strategy is chosen before anyone is in sight. Leadership is what happens when the strategy turns out to be wrong.

Strategy - before the battle

A plan: what the company is trying to do, on what ground, against what the council believes it is facing. Declared before contact and checked once.

  • Costs 1 action, or 2 if the plan requires ground to be scouted, prepared or occupied first.
  • Scored on planning - reading ground, knowing what a body of men can actually do, anticipating the other side.
  • Score it against what is being planned against. A conventional enemy on known ground is one demand; something with no doctrine behind it - a thing out of a vault, a crew who do not fight the way soldiers fight - is a different one, and a doctrine-bound planner is worth less there. This is where slow to improvise bites, and it bites before the battle rather than during it.
  • On success it grants a standing modifier of +1 to +3 to every exchange, by the margin.
  • It is built on assumptions, and the assumptions are written down.

Adaptation - during the battle

When the battle departs from the plan - the enemy is not who they were believed to be, the ground gives way, a flank that was secure is not - the commander must adapt, and that is a separate check.

Adaptation is not the commander having a better idea. It is whether the company still does what it is told while the plan is visibly failing. Orders given in the open, to frightened men, by someone they will obey.

  • Scored on command presence - being obeyed under pressure. Steadiness, clarity, and whether the rankers know the voice.
  • Succeed: the strategy modifier holds, or is replaced by a new one.
  • Fail: the strategy modifier is lost for the rest of the battle. The orders are no longer reaching the line, and the company fights on whatever it has left.

Personal flexibility is not what is being scored. Brackwater is adaptable in any conditions and frightens people, and is a foreigner in a city that votes - she is a poor adapter here, because two hundred and fifty men do not know her voice and will not move on it. Garrow is cold under fire and trusted by rankers, and is excellent at it.

Garrow is simply good at war, at both halves of it, and the council's problem is not that he is uneven - it is that he is one man with three actions. A battle he attends is a week he is not spending on anything else, and a battle he cannot attend is commanded by somebody the Watch has no reason to obey. That is Garrow's version of Partridge's single point of failure, and the council has not yet had to look at it.

The exchange

Both sides roll:

d10 + leadership + equipment + ground + odds + strategy

The loser loses cohesion:

cohesion lost = 5 + (margin x 3)

A tie costs nobody anything. Two bodies of men have shouted at each other.

Equipment is a flat modifier and also decides what the company can impose - pikes demand a different fight from bows, the same way an enemy's background sets the demand in party combat.

Ground decides who sets the terms, exactly as preparation does in party combat. Taking it is usually the objective, and it can change hands mid-battle.

Strength as a floor on what is possible

Absolute numbers decide what a company can be asked to do at all - hold a wall, cover a road, storm a place, picket a city of twenty-two thousand. A hundred men cannot do what two hundred and fifty can, whatever their cohesion.

Below half its established strength a company's ceiling drops by 20 (see below). A half-destroyed company does not come back to steady by resting.

What that means for Needleford today

The Watch stands at -19. Its ceiling is 41 - breaking.

The council cannot presently field the Watch against anybody. However long they rest, however well led, they cap out at a band where they will not advance and one bad exchange routs them. Nothing fixes that except paying them and being worth following.

Nobody designed that. It is eight weeks of arrears and one audit, read back as a number.

When the line breaks

If the company routs, or the line is broken around a councillor, it becomes party combat. Same beats, same acts, same three-level cap, with the opposition set by whatever got through.

A councillor who went to direct a battle is now in one.

Strength, pay and recruiting

Tracked live in ../ledger/the-watch.md.

The council sets the Watch's size and its wage, and bankrolls both. It may build whatever army it can afford. The payroll is headcount times wage, every tick, and it is the largest line in the cost base.

Recruiting

Governed by time. Standing and pay set the rate.

recruits per tick = (standing + 10) / 5,  rounded
                  + 2 per extra gold per week on the wage

Desertion

losses per tick = 1 per 2 full weeks of wages in arrears

Both run every tick, and the net can be negative. At -19 standing and eight weeks owed, the Watch recruits nobody and loses four men a week. It is shrinking, and it will go on shrinking until it is paid.

A faction that is owed money and asked to fight is not a resource. It is a creditor with weapons.

Dials

Provisional, and the first things to turn. Logged in ../infra/calibration-log.md as they are.

  • 60 + standing for the ceiling. The single most consequential number here - it decides how good a council has to be to have an army.
  • 5 + margin x 3 for cohesion loss, which sets how many exchanges a battle runs.
  • The odds table, and whether 2:1 should be worth more than 3.
  • Rout casualties at 20-30%.
  • The recruiting and desertion rates, which have never been tested because the Watch has never been anything but 250.

Open

  • One company or several. Written for one. Several would give real manoeuvre and a great deal more bookkeeping; added only if a battle wants it.
  • Sieges. A siege is not a sequence of exchanges and should not be forced into one.

Party Combat

Five individuals, or a subset of them plus any named NPCs with them, against a group. A dungeon. An ambush laid. An ambush walked into.

Not the rule for armed men against armed men - see mass combat, when it is written. Not the rule for a pursuit; the chase is parked until a chase happens, because it wants a map and inventing the map first is how you build a mechanic nothing fits.

The health ladder

From character-sheets.md, and it applies to every named character on either side:

healthy - injured - bloodied - dying - dead

A single fight may take a given character down at most three levels.

  • A healthy councillor cannot be killed in one fight. Healthy plus three is dying, not dead.
  • A councillor who walks in already hurt can be. Injured plus three is dead.

Going in wounded is the danger. The second raid in a fortnight is the one that kills somebody, and the council will have to choose between tempo and bodies knowing it.

The beat

A fight is a run of beats. Both sides roll every beat and the rolls are compared.

party:  d10 + the lead's fit        against
enemy:  d10 + their quality
Party wins the beatone hit on the enemy
Enemy wins the beatone hit on the party - a wound, one level
A tienothing. Two people have swung at each other and both are still standing

Who gets hit

Whoever won the beat chooses the target - and the choice must be narratively sensible.

A brute cannot reach Thackeray while Garrow is between them. He goes through Garrow or he goes nowhere. Equally, the party cannot put a blade in the enemy mage at the back merely because he is the one they would like to kill.

So shielding is not nominated, it is positional. Standing in front of somebody is a fact about where people are, not a declaration made at the moment of the wound - and it holds until somebody changes it.

Which makes position the thing worth fighting over. Putting a man on the ground is not only a hit; it opens a lane that was closed. The beat that knocks the front rank down is the beat that lets the next one reach the mage.

There is no difficulty number in party combat. The enemy is scored the same way a councillor is - -2 to +3 against what the beat demands, read off what they are. A trained housebreaker working by stealth is +3 when the beat demands stealth and poor when somebody sets the room on fire.

How a fight ends

  • The enemy breaks after N hits, where N is their headcount adjusted for quality. This is the same headcount-as-health the far side already runs on.
  • The party breaks when it decides to, or when nobody is left who can absorb a wound without being killed by it.

A fight the council is winning ends early. A fight it is losing ends when it admits it. Neither side has a hit-point sheet - one side has bodies and the other has a health ladder, and both were already written down.

The three-level cap forces damage to spread

Garrow can stand in front until he is dying. Then somebody else has to bleed. A long fight cannot be absorbed by one willing body, and when nobody is left who can take a hit without dying of it, the party is out of road and must break off.

Order of play

Commit-first, as everywhere else.

  1. The party commits - who leads this beat, who absorbs a loss, whether an act is being spent.
  2. Then the demand, the lead's fit and the enemy's quality are declared.
  3. Then both rolls.

Acts - one each, for the whole fight

Every named character gets exactly one act per fight. Councillors, and named NPCs on either side. Unnamed bodies are headcount and nothing else.

The skill is timing, not inventory. Thackeray's ward is precious because she cannot do it twice, and holding it for the beat that needs it is the entire decision.

What an act can do - the four dials

A fight has exactly four parameters, and an act turns one of them. There is no spell list. What a character may reach is read off their sheet, the same way a fit is.

DialWhat it looks like
Our rollBrackwater's alchemy, a position taken, a weapon prepared
Their rollsmoke, a ward against enemy magic, blinding, fouling the ground
HitsGarrow putting men down - a hit scored directly, without a beat
Positionopening a lane, closing one, getting between two people, forcing a line to break. Decides who can be hit at all
The demand, or their preparationfiring the fortification they dug into; dragging a stealth fight into the open where stealth is worth nothing

Hits and position overlap on purpose. Putting a man down removes a body and opens the ground he was standing on, which is why knocking people over is worth an act and not merely a flourish.

Healing mid-fight is our roll or hits depending on the fiction - a level restored is a level the party can still absorb, and absorbable levels are the party's real stamina.

Ongoing effects, and negating them

An act may leave something standing: smoke, a ward, fouled ground, a fire.

  • An ongoing effect lasts at least one full beat. It cannot be pre-empted.
  • After that it may be negated by the other side spending an act - magic wind clears a smokescreen, a dispel takes down a forcefield.
  • Negating costs the enemy their act, which is the trade. A ward that forces a trained enemy to spend his one act dispelling it has already paid for itself.

This is why the enemy having acts matters. Every named enemy can answer, and every answer is a thing they are not doing to the party instead.

Preparation - who chose the ground

One axis, three rungs. The defender holds the ground unless the attacker takes it from them.

Dug inknew it was coming and chose the ground. Sets what every beat demands, and +1 to their beat rolls
Readyknew, with no time to arrange. A straight fight
Surprisedcaught cold. Loses the first beat with no roll - somebody is hurt before anyone has decided anything - and the other side sets the demand throughout

This is already being tracked

Preparation is the detection state. Every notice roll in investigations.md has been quietly buying the far side a rung on this ladder since tick 1, and the council has never been told.

It cuts both ways. Preparation is purchasable: surveillance, a scouting pull, a records query that gives the ground before anyone walks it. Investigation is no longer only the mystery half of the game - it is what decides whether a fight is survivable.

Breaking off

Available between any two beats.

  • The party takes no further beats.
  • One member, chosen at random, takes a hit that cannot pass dying. Retreat is not free and it is not fatal.
  • They lose the objective, and the far side learns what they came for, which is often the more expensive half.

Death

A councillor can die. It is on the table and it is not softened.

A councillor's death is a guaranteed halt - unconditional, exempt from the halt budget, and already an irreversibility-4 trip-wire under simulation.md.

There is no one to replace them. Losing one is a catastrophe, not a staffing problem.

Recovery

Dying

  • Dying costs all three actions. A dying councillor does nothing.
  • Dying does not become dead inside the tick it happened. A full tick must pass, start to finish. Hit in tick N, they cannot die before the end of N+1.
  • Anyone may stabilise them. A check, d10 + fit, against a high difficulty. Brackwater has alchemy. Garrow has seen a great many wounds. Keel is useless.
  • Partridge cannot fail it. He is a superb surgeon and this is what that means on the page.
  • Success moves them to bloodied, and costs the treater one action. The patient has none to spend.
  • Treated in tick N they lose that tick's actions. Missed, they lose N and N+1 - six actions - and are dead at the end of it.

Healing does not happen on its own. A councillor who does not spend the action does not mend, however quiet the week.

Three levels is everything. The worst a non-dying councillor can be is bloodied, so one action each restores anyone to healthy with a level to spare. Partridge may do it three times in a tick, to three different people, or twice on himself.

What this means, and it is the point

The cost of a fight is no longer weeks. It is Partridge's week.

He does not heal faster per action - he removes the ceiling, and the action economy around him is a different game.

Which makes him the single point of failure, hard. If Partridge is the one dying, nobody auto-stabilises him, and the entire council drops to one level a tick with a rest action apiece. Taking Partridge out first is the correct opening move for any enemy who understands this council, and he is the most beloved and most exposed man in the city.

That is not an oversight. It is the design.

Petitions and Requests

How a faction makes the council do something it did not choose to do.

The ladder

Nothing here needs authoring. The council's own conduct manufactures its opposition, in four steps:

  1. A faction at standing 0 or above may make a polite request.
  2. Refusing a request costs nothing in actions, and a little standing.
  3. Enough refusals carry the faction below 0.
  4. Below 0, it petitions - and a petition cannot be waved away.

A council that is pleasant to deal with never sees a petition. A council that is busy, or correct, or merely tired, will see a great many.

Requests - for factions at 0 or above

  • Made in person or by letter. No signatures, no threshold, no clock.
  • Conceding or refusing costs no action. It is a conversation, not an instrument.
  • Refusal costs -1 to -3 standing, by the size of the ask.
  • Repeated refusal is how a friendly faction stops being one.

Petitions - for factions below 0

Who may raise one

Any faction, sub-faction or organisation: a bloc, a guild, a ward, a company, a crew.

It requires 80% of those who could sign. Stroud's petition carried nine of eleven warehousemen - 82% - and she stated plainly that the two who did not sign were not asked.

That threshold is a defensive lever. A divided faction cannot petition at all, so splitting one is a legitimate counter-move: buy, promote or frighten enough members and the thing collapses below 80% before it is ever delivered. Doing so is visible, and is its own kind of answer.

Power - what a faction may ask for

Each faction has a power of 1-10, recorded with its standing and its log in ../ledger/factions.md. It is strength, not mood: the guilds are powerful whether or not they like the council. Power caps the weight of the demand, on the same 1-10 scale as favours.

A faction cannot demand more than its power. A ward of fishwives may ask for its stall dues back. It may not ask for the harbour guard to be disbanded.

Pressure - how a petition forms

Petitions take time to assemble. Each tick a faction accumulates pressure toward the 80% it needs, from 0:

gain per tick = ( 10 + 5 x urgency + max(0, -standing) / 2 ) / size factor
  • urgency 0-4, how pressing the grievance is
  • standing the faction's current gauge value
  • size factor 1.0 for a small body, up to 1.5 for a whole bloc

Size cuts both ways. A large faction is slower to reach 80% because there are more people to convince - and what it may demand when it arrives is correspondingly larger.

Worked: the nobles and guilds at -14, urgency 2, size factor 1.3, gain about 20 points a tick. Four ticks to a petition.

The clock

A delivered petition names a deadline. Three ticks is standard. A demand of weight 7 or above gets four or five, because nobody expects the council to restructure the toll in a fortnight.

What a petition demands is always mechanical - move a rate, repeal a clause, dismiss an officer, open a yard, fund a thing. Conceding executes it.

The four answers

CostsEffect
CONCEDE1 actionThe demand is executed. The faction owes a favour, weight scaled to the ask.
ANSWER IN PART1 action, 1 tickGrant some, refuse some, and talk them into it. A check. On success it lands exactly as a concession

CONCEDE

The council may bank the favour rather than take the goodwill - that choice is theirs and should be made in the scene. Conceding to a hostile faction is the only way in the simulation to be owed by someone who dislikes you.

REFUSE

Costs standing by the weight of the demand. It is still an answer, and is always cheaper than silence.

IGNORE

The heavy one. Standing falls further than a refusal and the pressure is not spent - the grievance stands at full strength and the next petition forms from where this one left off. Ignoring compounds.

ANSWER IN PART

Formerly INSPECT. Hunting the flaw is no longer an answer in itself - it is one of the grounds you may stand on while doing this.

The council grants some of what was asked, refuses the rest, and persuades the petitioner to accept that as the answer. It is a check, resolved exactly like a thread pull: d10 + fit against a difficulty, per investigations.md.

  • Costs 1 action and one tick of the clock.
  • On success: it resolves as a CONCEDE. The petitioner is as satisfied as if the whole thing had been granted - pressure to 0, and a favour owed, weight scaled to what was actually conceded, not to what was asked.
  • On failure: nothing happens. No standing moves. The clock has run, the action is spent, and the council must still CONCEDE, ANSWER IN PART again, REFUSE or IGNORE.
  • Every failed attempt on the same petition adds +1 to the difficulty of the next one, cumulative, whoever does the talking.

There is no danger in pushing back. There is only time, and a three-tick clock holds about two attempts.

Declare the ground

Before the roll, the council says what it is standing on. The ground decides whose fit is scored, which is the whole decision - a council picks the hill it is good at, or the hill that is true, and those are not always the same.

GroundWhat it isCosts

Keel is +3 on law and poor on candour. Partridge is the reverse. The ground is chosen before anyone knows whether it was the right one.

The order of play

This ordering is load-bearing and is not optional.

  1. The council commits its speakers and their grounds.
  2. The fits and the difficulty are then declared, in the transcript.
  3. Then the roll.

The council chooses who stands up before it is told how well they will do. Everything below depends on that: if the scores were visible first, the council would send the one right person every time and a second speaker would never be worth an action.

Two speakers

The council may send up to two. Not three - a delegation stops being a conversation.

  • Two speakers costs two actions each. Four actions, out of fifteen, on top of the tick off the clock. One speaker costs one.
  • Each may stand on a different ground. Keel on law, Garrow on candour, in the same room.
  • Take the best of the two fits, scored each against their own ground.
  • +1 if both are positive, on whichever grounds they chose.
  • The weaker speaker never drags the result down. A bad choice wastes two actions; it does not make things worse than sending nobody.

Four actions is the point. A pair is not the sensible default, it is what a council buys when it is frightened - and a council answering two petitions in the same week cannot afford a pair for both. The second speaker is insurance against having picked the wrong hill, bought before anyone knows which hill was right.

A councillor who would be a liability in that room is still a liability in that room. The fit is scored from the sheets against the ground, and the fiction is not obliged to be kind about who was standing there.

Difficulty - how much is being refused

Base, by the share of the petition's weight being refused - weight, not clause count, because one clause is often the entire petition:

RefusedDifficulty
A sliver - one small point among many3
A corner - a quarter or so5
A third to a half7
Most of it9

Above three-quarters there is no check. Refusing nearly everything and calling it a partial answer is a refusal with a covering letter, and the petitioner will say so in those words. Use REFUSE.

Then modify:

The refused part is something the council genuinely cannot grant, and this can be shown-2
The council offers something not demanded that answers the real grievance-2
The council brings evidence the petitioner did not have-1
The refused part is the reason the petition exists+2
Each previous failed attempt on this petition+1

The -2 for offering something unasked-for is the strongest modifier in the table, and it is there on purpose. A petition is a statement of a grievance, not a list of its only remedies. A council that hears what the document is actually about can answer it with something that was never written down, and that is the most valuable thing this mechanic can teach.

Worked: the Watch's petition, tick 4. Three clauses; the refused one was a quarter of the weight (5), genuinely ungrantable under the charter and demonstrably so (-2), was the reason the petition existed (+2), and the council volunteered a standing undertaking nobody had asked for that answered the real grievance (-2). Difficulty 3. Ground: law, which is Keel's.

Formal requests upward

The council may not petition. It may write a formal request to the Crown or to the League of Kingdoms.

There must be a real reason for either to intervene. Both have their own interests, neither is obliged, and a request that is refused is remembered by the body that refused it.

Rules of the Simulation

The clock

One tick = one week of world time.

The simulation runs forward on its own. The heroes decide and act in character without supervision.

What stops the clock

Three things, and only three:

1. Severity

Something large or irreversible is about to land. The halt presents:

  • the situation, and
  • the heroes' own verdict, already reached.

The DM is not choosing what the heroes do. The DM rules on what the world does to them - adjusting external pressure, vetoing on lore grounds, or waving it through. The heroes' verdict stands unless overruled.

2. Adjudication

The simulation has hit a question the rules do not answer. Pure mechanics, no character decisions. A first-class halt, so that the rules grow deliberately rather than by improvisation.

3. Quiet

Nothing big has happened for too long. The council writes a despatch to its charter-holders - an in-world artifact that doubles as the DM's review document. This guarantees a regular chance to nudge even in calm weather.

Severity score

Four dimensions, each scored 0-4, then weighted. Anchors are fixed so that scoring stays consistent from tick to tick rather than drifting with mood.

Scope and cost are deliberately light. Spending the council's money, standing and favours is the heroes' own business. The DM is not asked to approve it.

The threshold

Halt at 14 or above.

Trip-wires

Regardless of the total:

  • Canon-risk of 3 or 4 always halts. This halt exists so the veto arrives before something is minted about the world, not after.
  • Irreversibility of 4 always halts. A thing nobody can undo stops the clock even when it is quiet, cheap and touches one person. A secret killing is the archetype.

Calibration

The threshold and the weights are tuned from DM reactions. Every halt is recorded in ../infra/calibration-log.md with its score and whether it deserved to stop.

Halt budget

Checkpoint period: 4 ticks - one month of world time.

Budget: 2 severity halts per period. If more qualify at once they arrive as one docket, not several interruptions. If more qualify than the cap allows, the lesser ones resolve on the heroes' own recommendation and are reported after the fact.

The trip-wires are exempt from the budget. A canon-risk 3+ or an irreversibility 4 always reaches the DM, however busy the month has been. Otherwise a crowded period could silently suppress precisely the veto the trip-wire exists to deliver.

Quiet halt: 4 ticks without any halt and the council writes a despatch.

In a bad month that is roughly one contact a fortnight. In a calm one, one a month.

The DM

The man behind the curtain. The DM:

  • controls external factors - what crises arrive, and when
  • tunes mechanics and answers adjudications
  • flags where the simulation diverges from the intended vision
  • may assume the persona of the Crown's agent when it suits, as a voice to pick up and put down

The DM does not order the heroes about. They are characters, not units.

What every halt carries

Besides the situation and the heroes' verdict, a halt carries:

Mechanic opportunities. Whenever play reaches something that wants a rule and does not have one - a raid on a hideout, mass combat, a riot with demands, a trial, a siege, a negotiation with real stakes - it is raised at the next halt for the DM to design with. The simulation does not quietly improvise a subsystem and present it as settled. Flagging is the default; silence is the exception.

Scenes

Every important decision gets a scene. Not a summary of a decision - a scene, with people in a room, in their own voices. Short is fine; two hundred words of Keel being correct at somebody is worth more than a paragraph reporting that he was.

Scenes are written for:

  • any decision the council actually has to make
  • anything interesting happening to a named character
  • every halt, so the DM rules on a moment rather than on a bullet point

Everything else - the ordinary running of the city - can be narrated.

Scenes are written to ../transcripts/.

Artifacts

The simulation produces things, not only prose. Maps, letters, manifests, charters, bills of lading, watch rosters, guild petitions, despatches, warrants, handbills nailed to a door.

If an object exists in the fiction and a person could hold it, it should probably exist on disk. These go in their own directories under simulation/ and are named as the world would name them.

The viewer

../infra/viewer/build.py renders everything human-readable under simulation/ into one self-contained page. No server, no dependencies, no build step - it opens straight off the filesystem.

It never reads infra/. The seals are excluded by construction, not by care.

Rebuild it at the end of every tick. The DM has the URL and does not need it repeated.

Canon

  1. ../../laurasia-worldbuilding/ - final say, always.
  2. ../canon/ - binding once written.
  3. In-the-moment invention - must contradict neither.

Anything deviating from (1) needs an in-world narrative reason and explicit approval.

Persistence

Every human-readable thing the simulation produces is written to the file system. Nothing of consequence lives only in conversation.

The seal, and collisions with it

The solution to the mission's mystery is fixed and written down in ../infra/sealed/the-culprit.md. The DM asked to be kept in the dark, on purpose. Nothing in ../infra/sealed/ is ever quoted, summarised, paraphrased or hinted at outside that folder. The heroes learn only what they earn through play. The DM may order the seal broken at any time - only then.

The DM also rules on whether a faction's behaviour makes sense for that faction. Those rulings can contradict the sealed solution, so:

When a DM ruling collides with the seal, say that it does - never what it collides with - and grade it:

  • Soft collision. Nothing the heroes have seen yet depends on the contradicted part. Re-seal quietly, adjust, say it was soft. The mystery stays fair and the DM need do nothing.
  • Hard collision. Clues already delivered would become lies, and the mystery becomes unsolvable or unfair. Say so explicitly and stop. This is the one case where a veto carries a cost the DM cannot see.

On a hard collision the DM has three doors: override (the solution is rebuilt around the ruling), unseal (decide knowingly), or defer (an in-world reason is found for the odd behaviour, which is the standing rule for deviation anyway).

Flagging a collision leaks a sliver - the DM learns that something they vetoed mattered. That is accepted. Silently breaking the mystery is worse.

Tenure - the Ash Clock

The threat is tenure, not theft. The canon is in ../canon/the-mission.md: disassembled the device is utterly inert, and assembled it bleeds ash whether anybody wants it to or not. Nobody has to detonate anything for this to become a catastrophe. Somebody only has to hold it together and wait.

This is the only mechanic in the simulation that makes doing nothing expensive.

The phases

The adversary cannot skip a phase and cannot run two at once. Each step is work, and work costs actions.

PhaseWhat it takesWhere it leaks
1. Recoveryone action per attempt; the vault is trapped and attempts can fail badlycorpses, hired replacements, cargo moving out of nowhere
2. Researchseveral actions; cannot be done in-housethey must ask someone who knows - a scholar, a text, a dwarf
3. Assemblyone action, once, and only with every piece in one placethe largest single movement of the operation
4. Generationfree, and automatic, foreverthe ash itself, which is heavy and has to go somewhere

Research cannot be done in-house. This is structural, not flavour. Nobody in a criminal crew knows how an unaccountable object fits together, so they have to reach outside themselves, and reaching outside is what makes threads. It is the phase where a careful council is most likely to catch them.

Recovery is dangerous

Whatever the pieces are kept behind was built to keep them. Each recovery attempt may fail, and a bad failure costs a body or a week or both. This slows the adversary honestly rather than by fiat, and a body is a lead.

Generation

Once assembled, the device produces one crate of ash per tick, forever, costing the adversary nothing. Switching it on is the last decision they ever have to make.

Ten crates is the line. That is enough ash to do catastrophic, permanent harm to ground that does not come back - whether it is shipped out, buried under a warehouse, or stacked where it stands. At ten crates the situation has passed from a recovery into a disaster, and the simulation changes mode rather than ending.

The crates leak

Generation is free. Keeping the ash is not. A crate is a physical object, and canon has the ash moving as cargo - through roads, quays, bonded storage and manifests, the same as anything else in this city.

So the adversary chooses, and both choices cost:

  • Move them as they come. One action per movement. Ten small traces: a carter, a bonded entry, a warehouseman who remembers an odd weight.
  • Let them stack. Free, but a growing pile in one place is conspicuous, and whatever is guarding it is guarding something.

Either way the council's invisible clock has ten visible shadows. The council loses this by missing ten chances, not by failing a count they were never shown.

Where the ash goes - a warehouse, a cellar, a mansion, a hull on the tide - is the adversary's decision, not the simulation's.

The adversary's actions

Every actor worth simulating has one action per tick, the adversary included. Covering tracks, recovering a piece, researching, moving ash, buying a favour - all the same single action. See investigations.md for covering and the burn.

This is the real constraint. A week spent hiding is a week the vault is not emptied; a week spent moving ash is a week nothing is learned. The adversary is never doing everything at once, and what they are not doing is where the council gets in.

Headcount

Headcount is the adversary's health. A crew whittled down works slower: below a threshold they drop to one action every other tick.

Reinforcements take three or four ticks to arrive and leave their own trail - people travel, people are paid, people are housed, and somebody notices strangers.

This gives the council a way to slow the clock without having solved anything, which is the mercy the rest of this design does not offer.

What the council can see

Nothing. There is no gauge, no counter, no warning. The ash clock is sealed and stays sealed.

What the council sees is a city where odd cargo moves, strangers arrive, a scholar is asked an unusual question, and a road nobody has used in years carries wheel-ruts. The clock is only ever legible through its shadows.

Panic and the endgame

The adversary is not a schedule. If the council gets close - heat in the city, lanterns in the dungeon - they may run, they may dig in and fight, they may move the whole operation somewhere worse. Where they assemble, whether they ship, and whether they stand are their decisions, taken in the moment from what they believe about the council's attention.

That belief is tracked in ../infra/sealed/the-operation.md, and it can be wrong.

Upkeep - Deferred Maintenance as Debt

Three lines in the cost base can be cut without anyone noticing inside the week. All three compound. Cutting them is cheap now and expensive later, by design - see economy.md.

This mechanic makes "later" a number.

Condition

Each upkeep line carries a condition, 0-100.

BandCondition
Sound75-100
Worn50-74
Failing25-49
Derelict0-24

What the council can see

Not the same for each line. This is the heart of the mechanic.

LineObvious without lookingOtherwise
Dredgingat 40 or belowgaugers and pilots know first; the council does not
Quays, roads, lampsat 20 or belowa rotten quay looks like a quay
Relief, fire, contingencyneverthere is nothing to see until the day there is

Above those thresholds the condition must be inspected - one action, a surveyor, a pilot, a walk along the lower quays - or learned by accident in play.

An inspection returns the true number. Not a band, not an impression - the exact condition, which the council may then reason with, budget against and argue over. There is no roll and no chance of a bad read: an action spent looking is an action that tells you what is there.

It tells them nothing about when. The event roll stays hidden, so a council that inspects knows precisely how bad it is and still cannot say whether it has a month or a week.

Relief and fire are never obviously in disrepair. That is what makes the smallest line the most dangerous one: it is the only place where cutting leaves no visible mark at all, right up until a fire is not contained.

How it moves

Full funding holds a line steady. It does not improve it. The cost base pays for keeping up, not catching up.

  • Underfunding drops condition by (share of the line not paid) x d[15-20] per tick - random, so the council can never budget its neglect precisely. Skipping dredging entirely is −15 to −20 a tick; paying half is −7 to −10.
  • Overfunding raises it, at the catch-up rate below.

The debt

Every gold not spent becomes arrears - work owed to the city. Arrears are not repaid at face value.

Catch-up multiplier: 2 + (consecutive ticks neglected / 4)

A week of skipped dredging saves 700G and costs 1,400G to put right. Skip it for two months and the same week costs 2,800G. Neglect is a loan at a punitive rate, taken out against a lender who does not negotiate.

Arrears are tracked in ../ledger/upkeep.md, and are not the treasury's Owed - nobody is waiting to be paid. The city is simply owed work.

What failure looks like

Dredging - 700G

The head of navigation silts. Usable draught falls and the largest hulls stop coming up.

At Failing, the passage toll freezes. It stops answering the rate. The council can raise the toll all it likes and the revenue does not move, because the hulls that would have paid the difference cannot get up the river. The city's chief revenue and chief political instrument both go dead at once, and the only thing that unfreezes them is a dredger.

Worse, the hysteresis in economy.md applies in full. Trade that reroutes because the channel shoaled does not come back when it is dredged, any more than it comes back when a toll is lowered.

Silting is a toll rise the council never voted for, and cannot repeal.

Below 25: the channel begins to change rather than merely fill. Some lost draught is permanent and no dredging restores it.

Quays, bridge, roads, lamps - 900G

Dark streets, bad roads, a bridge that closes to waggons.

This line feeds the chance of robbery. Money moving through a city with unlit wards and broken roads is money moving slowly in the dark. The rules for that sit with raids and physical action; this is the dial that drives them.

Below 25: something falls down that is not rebuilt in this campaign.

Relief, fire, contingency - 400G

The first thing anyone proposes cutting, the smallest line on the page, and the only one with no warning signs at all.

Failure is a fire that is not contained, or a winter the river wards do not eat through. It costs standing with the citizenry and the river wards - the two gauges the council currently holds in credit, and therefore the two it has furthest to fall from.

And a fire burns things. A failure on this line can take condition off the quays-and-roads line on top of the standing loss. Neglecting the cheapest line is how a council damages the expensive one without ever deciding to.

Below 25: the city stops expecting anything from the council - a standing floor that money cannot buy back.

The hidden part

Visibility is per-line, above. The event roll is never visible. The council may know the quays are failing; they never know which week a quay gives way. That is enough - the dread does the work.

Windfalls

Every other mechanic in this simulation is a way to lose. Traffic falls, standing decays, threads go cold, collectors skim, maintenance rots, ash accrues, winter crosses zero.

Windfalls are the only way to win a week. One-off bursts of standing, favour or gold, arriving outside the usual channels, and gone if not taken in the tick they appear.

Two sources

Luck, which is not luck

Standing-based. People bring their problems to a council they trust. A lady at the chamber door, a letter, a warning about a robbery that has not happened yet.

This is the only place in the design where positive standing does something. Everything else keys off the negative - skim chance, petitions, withholding. A council at +18 with the citizenry has, until now, been paid nothing for it.

One roll a tick, against the pool. Not one roll per faction - a single roll for whether, then a weighted draw for who.

1. The pool. Sum the standing of every faction in credit. Remote and institutional factions - the Crown, the League, the sea factors - are quartered into the sum. They are not people who walk up to a councillor in the street; what they offer is a commission, and it is rarer.

2. The roll. chance = pool x 1.0%, capped at 60%.

3. The source, if it hits: drawn at random, weighted by each faction's contribution to the pool. A faction at +5 is still the source one time in twenty. Someone the council barely knows occasionally knocks, which is truer than the best-liked faction winning every week.

The source is drawn, not chosen. What is offered follows from who is offering.

These always arrive at the start of the tick, with the full action budget still in hand. The council is never denied one by the clock.

Skill

Earned. The investigation mechanic in investigations.md grades margin and pays an intel bonus at margin 3+. At margin 6 or above the council may take that bonus material instead: they did not only find the lead, they found what was under it.

It replaces the intel bonus, never adds to it, and it is available only where the fiction already supplies something material. Both conditions are in investigations.md and both are load-bearing.

For a competent, well-matched pair at the default difficulty this is one pull in five. Deliberately the same order as the standing roll, so neither channel becomes the one the council lives on.

Negotiations, inspections and audits can do the same - the other party, well handled, mentions a thing they need done.

No separate roll. Frequency is governed by how often the council does things well, which is the correct governor.

And by what they are willing to give up. The choice at margin 6 is between a lead and a payday, on a thread that is 10 weaker either way. Every earned windfall in this simulation is a week the council chose money over the mission.

These arrive when they arrive - mid-tick, attached to the action that earned them. If a councillor's last action generates one, it is lost unless somebody else still has an action to spend.

That is deliberate. A council that spends every councillor down in parallel can never catch a windfall, so keeping somebody in reserve becomes a real strategy - and it costs them tempo on the mission to do it.

Taking one

A windfall costs an action to claim. This is the difference between a windfall and income: income is passive and arrives whether the council acts or not. A windfall is an errand, and an errand is a thing somebody does.

Scale

Roughly half a week of the Watch:

KindSize
Gold~1,250G
Favourweight 3-5
Standing+3 to +6

Enough to matter against an 8,000G week. Not enough to be a plan.

Not all of them are free

A windfall with no risk is not a decision, it is a button. If every errand is pure upside the council takes all of them reflexively, and the mechanic is only slower income after all.

Some are straightforwardly good. Others are errands with teeth - stopping a robbery, a difficult negotiation, a confiscation whose owner wants it back. These are resolved exactly like a thread pull: the demand is named, the councillors in play are scored against it from their sheets, and the result follows the margin. See investigations.md.

Failure costs what the fiction says it costs - injury, standing from a botched negotiation, a favour spent to get out of it.

Resolving a windfall never triggers another windfall. Whatever the margin.

Consequences outlast the windfall

No windfall itself lasts past the tick. Its consequences do.

A robbery not prevented is a shop robbed, and a shopkeeper who remembers. A stash of weapons confiscated is somebody looking for their weapons. A negotiation gone well is a person who now expects the council to be reasonable next time, and a negotiation gone badly is the reverse.

Every windfall taken or refused writes its consequence into the world, the same as anything else.

Authoring

The simulation does not invent windfalls outside these rules. Not for now.

Adding one because a scene seemed to earn it is right for story and is also the single place in this design where the simulation could quietly make the game easier every time things felt bad. The DM will mark the points where a windfall should have appeared, and the rules are calibrated from that.

Every windfall is logged with its source - rolled, earned, or authored - in ../ledger/windfalls.md, so the rate is visible and can be argued with.

The rate, and why the cap is there

At the Year 0 standings the pool is 26.75 - the citizenry at 18, the Crown quartered to 7.5, the League quartered to 1.25 - giving 27%, or a windfall roughly every fourth week.

The cap is the promise. A pooled roll grows linearly with how liked the council is, and without a ceiling a council that wins over four more blocs would be fielding errands most weeks. Capped at 60%, fortune knocks more often as they earn it and never becomes the thing they live on.

Dials, in the order they should be turned: the 1.0% per point, then the 60% cap, then the quartering of remote factions. Recorded in ../infra/calibration-log.md.

The First Sitting

Tick 1, Year 0. The chamber above the customs house, because the council has nowhere else yet.

The room smelled of tar. It was directly over the bonded sheds, and nobody had thought to ask whether that mattered before the charter was read out in it.

Ambrose Keel had the books open before the others had sat down.

"Three rates," he said. "Passage, warehousing, market dues. The charter lets us set all three. The previous arrangement set them in the year the quay was rebuilt and nobody has touched them since, which means the city has been governed for thirty years by a dead man's guess."

"Start with what we need," said Alder Garrow.

"Two hundred and fifty watchmen, paid badly and late. Wages are nine weeks in arrears." Keel did not look up. "That is the whole of the problem. Everything else is preference."

Ines Thackeray said, "Then raise the toll."

"No." Garrow and Keel said it together, which neither of them enjoyed.

Keel recovered first. "The toll is why anyone comes here. Raise it and the hulls stop at Oldhithe and lighter up on the tide, and the carts go round by Thrushford and ford it badly, and then we have the same wages owing against a smaller city. Both of those are worse than us. Neither of them is worse than us by very much."

The Muster Cycle

Tick 3. The chamber, late, with the lamps going.

"The money runs through a factor because a factor is how you move it. That's not what it is. That's what it's wearing."

The Auctioneer Has Forgotten

Tick 4. A room off the Hithe, second time.

He was charming about it. That was the first thing wrong.

The first time Thackeray had come to him he had been brusque and precise and faintly insulted by the question, which is what a man is like when he remembers something exactly and is being asked whether he remembers it. This time he was warm. He offered her tea. He said that there had been so very many pieces through this room, and that a year was a long time, and that he would hate to say a thing that turned out to be wrong and send the council off down a road that did not go anywhere.

She asked him nine questions and he gave her nine hedges.

"You have remembered this better before," she said at last.

"Have I? Then I was less careful before."

She did not get what she came for. The nine pieces are gone out of the record of him; whatever he once knew about where the lot went after it left his room is now a man being careful.

She got something else, and she got it because she had stopped asking about the jewellery and started asking about him.

A man had been to see him. Nine or ten days ago. Not a buyer - he had bought nothing and looked at nothing. That night, with Brackwater:

"Back stair. From the lane. Nine or ten days."

Brackwater put her own notebook on the table and opened it to the page she had written in the second week, sitting in a doorway opposite that lane, and turned it round.

Same day. Same hour. Same stair.

"I have been following him since Tuesday," she said.

What the Petition Asked

Tick 4. The chamber, mid-morning.

Roper came in uniform, with his helmet under his arm, and did not sit when he was offered a chair.

"Two hundred and thirteen of two hundred and fifty, sir," he said to Garrow, and then corrected himself and said it to the council, because that was the proper way of it and Roper had been nineteen years learning the proper way of things. "Eighty-five in the hundred."

Keel took the pages. He read them the way he reads everything, which is to say he read the third clause first.

"You have asked us to give up our audit."

"We've asked you to give it to somebody who doesn't owe us money."

"The charter vests the inspection of revenue in this council. It does not vest it anywhere else, and it does not permit this council to vest it anywhere else." Keel set the page down. "You are asking us for a thing we may not lawfully grant, Sergeant."

Roper had clearly expected this, and had clearly been told by somebody that this was what Keel would say.

"Then say that," he said. "Out loud, at the four places, with the schedule. Say you won't do it and why. That'll do us nearly as well, because the city will have read it."

There was quite a long silence.

"That is a better instrument than the one you have written," said Keel.

"I know," said Roper. "Mr Keel - nobody in that yard thinks you're a thief. They think you looked at them like one and then went quiet about it. The quiet is the whole of it. You could have fixed this for the price of a sheet of paper and you didn't, and now it's cost you a petition."

After he had gone:

"He is right," said Keel.

"He is," said Garrow.

"I want that in the minute. He is right, and I was correct, and the two are not the same thing, and I have now done it twice in four weeks."

The Verdict

Tick 4. The chamber, end of the week. Five people and one sheet of paper.

"Clause one and clause two are not in dispute," Keel began. "We owe it, we have it, and a schedule costs us nothing we were not already going to spend. I object to neither."

"Then say so and sit down," said Partridge.

"Clause three is the petition." Keel put his hand flat on it. "We cannot grant it. Not will not - cannot. The charter vests inspection of revenue in this council. A clause purporting to vest it in a Crown officer is a cession of chartered authority, and a council that gives away a power the Crown gave it discovers quite quickly that the Crown gave it to the council for a reason."

"So we refuse it," said Garrow, "and two hundred and fifty men hear that the council kept the right to search them and didn't give them anything back."

"Yes. Unless we give them what Roper actually asked for, which is not what is written on the page."

Brackwater looked up. She had not spoken in forty minutes.

"The quiet," she said.

"The quiet." Keel almost looked pleased. "He told us plainly. Nobody in that yard believes I am a thief-taker. They object to being searched in silence and cleared in silence. The grievance is not the audit. It is that an audit of the Watch is an event the city hears about and an acquittal of the Watch is not."

What the council resolved, five to nothing:

Clause one - conceded. A schedule of the eight weeks, with a date against each, posted at the four places.

Clause two - conceded. The first payment made within the week. Two thousand five hundred gold, against a chest of six thousand two hundred and fifty-seven.

Clause three - refused, in the posted document, with the reason stated in full: that the council may not give away a power it holds under charter, and that it would be lying to the Watch if it pretended otherwise.

And, not asked for: that this council will publish the result of any inspection of any collector of the city's revenue - the Watch, the harbour guard, the warehousemen, the guilds - within seven days, whether it finds anything or not, at the same four places. Beginning with the inspection of the third week, which found nothing, and which is to be posted tomorrow.

"You understand what you have just written," said Thackeray.

"I have written that we will never again audit anyone quietly."

"Yes."

"I do." Keel capped the ink. "I have spent four weeks explaining to this council that a thing can be correct and still cost more than it is worth. It appears I have been listening to myself."

Garrow read it over twice.

"They'll take it," he said.

"They will take it," Keel agreed. "That is not the same as it being wise, and I would like that in the minute as well."
